## Epic CEO Criticizes Steam's AI Disclosure Labels as "Irresponsible"

**Tim Sweeney** has openly criticized Valve's mandatory AI disclosure tags on Steam, calling them a "Scarlet Letter" that puts developers at a disadvantage. In an interview with PC Gamer, the Epic Games CEO argued that these labels make it "much, much, much harder for a game developer to have a chance of success" by exposing them to organized backlash from anti-AI communities.

Sweeney's stance reflects a growing divide in the industry: while some platforms and consumers demand transparency about AI usage, major engine providers like Epic are doubling down on AI integration—Unreal Engine 6 will feature even deeper AI support. His argument that AI tools are simply "productivity-improving tools" comparable to asset stores or photoscanning reveals the tension between creative empowerment and consumer trust in an era where generative AI is becoming ubiquitous in game production.

## CD Projekt Red CEO Confirms AI-Generated Games Are Coming, But Questions Their Quality

**Michał Nowakowski**, joint CEO of CD Projekt Red, has confirmed that fully AI-generated games are in active development. In a conversation with Edge's Knowledge newsletter, he recounted meeting studio founders who claim they can produce 40 prototypes in a week, select the best five, and launch a game within three weeks—a production cycle that stands in stark contrast to CDPR's years-long development philosophy.

While acknowledging the technical feasibility, Nowakowski expressed significant doubts about whether this volume-driven approach represents a sustainable path forward. His skepticism carries weight given CDPR's reputation for handcrafted worlds and narrative depth. The real concern isn't that AAA studios will adopt this model, but rather platform saturation: Steam already struggles with discoverability, and a flood of AI-generated content could bury quality titles under mountains of "AI slop."

## EA Executive Reports "Real Rise of Creativity" from AI Adoption

**Laura Miele**, EA's president of enterprise development, has offered a notably optimistic perspective on AI's impact at one of the world's largest game publishers. Speaking at Game Business Live during Summer Game Fest, Miele stated that AI has enabled "faster prototyping, faster creativity, and shorter conversations around creativity and coming to alignment" across EA's studios.

Her comments stand in contrast to internal reports of employee discontent and concerns about job security. Miele frames AI as a friction-removal tool that eliminates tedium rather than replacing creative workers—a narrative that aligns with EA CEO Andrew Wilson's earlier declaration that AI is "the very core of our business." However, with over 100 active AI projects at EA and reports of employees being asked to train AI on their own work, the line between augmentation and replacement remains contested.

## Veteran Developer: AI Won't Replace Human Creativity for 20+ Years, But Could Spark a Renaissance

**Rich Vogel**, a veteran game developer with decades of experience, has offered a nuanced perspective on AI's role in game development. In a LinkedIn post, Vogel argued that while AI will become "entrenched in the overall process," the core challenge of "finding the fun" remains too complex for AI to replicate for at least the next two decades.

Vogel predicts AI will primarily impact production pipelines—shaders, textures, animations, rigging, and concepting—while most assets will still be developed by humans. His most intriguing prediction: when AI enables genuinely new emergent gameplay that has never been seen before, "that is when the true Renaissance in game development begins." This perspective offers a middle ground between AI alarmism and uncritical adoption, emphasizing that the technology's ultimate value lies not in replacing human creativity but in unlocking entirely new creative possibilities.
